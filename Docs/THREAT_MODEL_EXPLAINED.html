Before anything elseWhat is a "threat model"?

It's a document that answers three questions, in order, before you write any code:

  • What am I protecting? (assets)
  • Who am I protecting it from? (attackers)
  • What can they actually do — and where do I give up? (threats and limits)
Analogy

You're securing a house. The amateur asks "how do I make it safe?" and buys a very expensive front door. The professional asks: what's actually valuable inside (the jewellery, not the sofa), who realistically comes (an opportunist trying handles, not a military unit), and what am I not going to stop (someone who owns the building's master key).

Then they discover the back window has a broken latch and fix that instead of upgrading the door. That's threat modelling. It stops you spending five months armouring the wrong thing.

The reason yours comes first, before the format spec and the code, is that every later design decision is an answer to a question this document asks. If you don't know who you're defending against, you can't tell whether a design is good.

Section 1The three walls you cannot get through

Your original goal was: once converted, the file cannot be edited or shared to any device. The document opens by saying that's impossible. Not "hard" — impossible. Here is why, and none of these is about you being a student or Rust not being good enough. They'd stop Google.

🚪

Wall 1 — The person you're protecting the file from is holding the computer

Your app runs on their machine. Every check it makes — "is this the right device?", "has this expired?" — is a line of code on hardware they fully control. They can change it.

📷

Wall 2 — The analog hole

For a person to read a document, it has to appear on a screen. Once it's on a screen, a phone camera can photograph it. No amount of encryption touches this.

📋

Wall 3 — Copying is free

A file is a sequence of bytes. On a general-purpose computer, Ctrl+C works on any sequence of bytes. You cannot make bytes uncopyable.

Wall 1 is the one that matters most, so let's slow down

Analogy — the bouncer who works for the burglar

Imagine hiring a bouncer to guard a door. Normally fine — the bouncer works for you and stands in the club you own.

Now imagine you must place that bouncer inside the burglar's own house, and the burglar pays his salary, feeds him, and can fire him at any moment. How much is his "sorry, you're not on the list" worth?

That's what your Decipher app is. It's a bouncer standing inside the attacker's house. This is the oldest and most reliable rule in the field: you cannot enforce rules on a computer you don't control.

Real proof that this is not theoretical: Netflix, Disney and Google spend enormous sums on DRM. Widevine — Google's system — has three security levels, because of exactly this problem. Its lowest level (L3, pure software) is broken; that's why you only get 480p when a browser can't prove it has protected hardware. Billions of dollars have not solved Wall 1. It isn't a budget problem.

The dotted line is the whole problem. Anything you send across it, they own.

So what do you do?

You stop trying to win a fight you can't win, and you change what you promise. This is the single most important paragraph in the whole document:

The reframe — your actual thesis statement

SafeIT doesn't stop copying. It makes a copy useless on any other device, makes any edit detectable, makes access expire and be cancellable, and makes any leak traceable to whoever leaked it.

All four are achievable, provable, and demonstrable live on a projector. Compare:

What you first saidWhat you'll actually claim
"Can't be shared"Copy it anywhere you like — it's meaningless bytes without an enrolled device and a live licence✓ provable
"Can't be edited"Change one bit and the file refuses to open, every time ✓ provable
"Always controlled"Access expires on schedule and the sender can kill it remotely ✓ provable
"Can't leak"If someone photographs their screen, the watermark says who ✓ provable
"Unbreakable"Costs a skilled reverse engineer serious time — and we say exactly how much⚠ honest
Why this earns marks rather than losing them

A bank vault door isn't sold as "impenetrable". It's rated TL-30: it resists a skilled attacker with tools for thirty minutes. That rating is why people trust it. A door marketed as "unbreakable" tells you the manufacturer either hasn't tested it or is lying.

Saying "here is precisely what I stop and precisely where I stop" is what a professional sounds like. Your examiner will have seen twenty projects claiming unbreakable encryption. Be the one that gives a rating.

The one to lose sleep over

A4, the signing key. Everything else is one file or one machine. A4 is the master seal: whoever holds it can mint files that every copy of your app on earth will accept as genuine. Notice the asymmetry — A2 being stolen loses one document; A4 being stolen loses the entire system, retroactively. That's why the plan says keep it offline.

Analogy — the same ladder, for a house

T0 the neighbour who tries the handle. T1 someone checking windows and looking under the doormat. T2 a professional burglar with lockpicks and an afternoon. T3 the building's owner, who has the master key and can knock down a wall. T4 a person across the street with binoculars.

A good lock stops T0 and T1 completely, costs T2 real time and risk, and is simply irrelevant to T3 and T4. Nobody thinks less of a lock for that. You install a camera for T4 — that's your watermark.

The sentence to put in your report

"SafeIT gives strong guarantees against T0 and T1, meaningfully raises the cost for T2, and provides attribution against T4. It does not claim to defeat T3."

Say that in the viva before anyone asks. It converts your biggest vulnerability — "sir, can't I just screenshot it?" — from a gotcha into evidence that you understood the problem.

Section 4STRIDE — a checklist so you don't forget a category

STRIDE is just six letters naming six ways things go wrong. It's a pilot's pre-flight checklist: its value isn't cleverness, it's that going through all six stops you from forgetting one.

S poofing

Pretending to be someone you're not. Using a fake ID at the door.

T ampering

Changing data you shouldn't. Adding a zero to a cheque.

R epudiation

Doing something, then denying it. "That's not my signature."

I nformation disclosure

Secrets leaking out. Papers falling from your bag.

D enial of service

Breaking it so nobody can use it. Gluing the lock shut.

E levation of privilege

Getting rights you weren't given. Visitor pass opens the CEO's office.

The four rows in that table people find confusing

1 · "Chunk transplant" / cut-and-paste attack

Your file is encrypted in pieces (chunks), like a contract split into numbered pages. An attacker takes page 5 out of a harmless document and glues it into a secret one, hoping the app won't notice.

The fix: stamp every page with "page 5 of 40, contract #1234" in a way that can't be peeled off — that's what "binding the container ID and chunk index into the AAD" means. Move the page anywhere and the stamp contradicts its surroundings, so it's rejected.

2 · "Rollback" the offline licence

Your licence is cached so the file works on a plane. So the attacker backs up that cache today, waits for it to expire next week, and restores the old copy — like restoring an old save file in a game to un-spend your money, or winding back a car's odometer.

The fix: a monotonic counter — a number that can only ever go up, kept in secure hardware. It's a ratchet: it turns one way. If the restored file says "5" and the ratchet is at "9", you know it came from the past, and you refuse.

3 · "Error messages as a decryption oracle"

The safe-cracking scene in every heist film: the attacker turns the dial and listens for a click. The click tells them digit one is right, so they move to digit two. Without the click they'd need to try every combination; with it, they crack it in minutes.

Your error messages are the click. "Wrong device" versus "Expired" versus "Bad signature" tells an attacker exactly which wall they hit, so they can work through them one at a time. The fix: one identical message for every failure, plus an opaque reference code you can look up in your own logs. Silence beats a helpful click.

4 · Length fields and "a 4 GB header will kill you"

Your file starts with a number saying "the header is N bytes long", and your program allocates N bytes of memory to hold it. An attacker writes N = 4,000,000,000. Your program obediently tries to allocate 4 GB and dies.

The fix: check every length against a hard limit before allocating anything. This is unglamorous, and it's where real file parsers get broken. Never trust a number that came out of a file.

The big oneWhy you must never write if (valid) { decrypt() }

If you take one idea from this entire project, take this one. It's the last row of the STRIDE table and it's the thing that makes SafeIT actually work.

Top: the design every student writes. Bottom: the design that survives Wall 1.

Analogy — two ways to run a nightclub

Design A: a bouncer checks your ticket and opens an unlocked door. The door was never locked; the bouncer is the security. Remove the bouncer — bribe him, distract him, or in software, change one instruction — and you walk in. Everyone who ever got in did so through an already-open door.

Design B: the door has a lock needing two keys turned together. You keep one. The ticket is the other. There is no bouncer to remove, because there was never a decision — just a lock that either opens or doesn't.

Design B is what "the licence is a key share, not a boolean" means. And it's why revocation genuinely works: stop issuing the second key and the door stops opening. Nothing on the attacker's machine can compensate, because the missing piece was never on their machine.

This is also, quietly, the answer to Wall 1. You can't stop them patching your code — so you build a system where patching your code gains them nothing, because the thing they need was never there to begin with.

Section 6Non-goals — saying what you don't do

Listing your limits up front feels like admitting weakness. It's the opposite, and here is the test: which surgeon do you trust — the one who says "this procedure carries a 3% risk of complications", or the one who says "totally safe, don't worry"?

The second one either hasn't measured, or is hiding something. Precision reads as competence.

The tactical reason

If you raise the limitation, it's scope. If the examiner raises it and you hadn't mentioned it, it's a flaw you missed. Same fact, completely different outcome. Get there first — every time.

Section 7The legal and ethical bit

Three short things, each worth a paragraph in your report:

  • Your device fingerprint is personal data. Disk serials and machine IDs identify a person's hardware, so India's DPDP Act and the EU's GDPR apply. Hence the design choice of hashing them with a secret salt — you can still recognise a returning device, but you never store anything reversible. Show that you knew that was a choice.
  • Circumventing DRM is separately regulated — India's Copyright Act §65A, the DMCA §1201 in the US. Your attack lab is on a system you built and own, which is fine. Saying so in one sentence shows you know the boundary exists.
  • Dual use. The same technology that protects a hospital's patient records also lets someone make an inconvenient document self-destruct. A lock doesn't know who's on which side of the door. Acknowledging that you built something with two faces is a maturity signal, and it costs you one paragraph.

FinallyThe five questions you will be asked

Rehearse these out loud

1. "Can't I just screenshot it?"

Yes — and no system on earth prevents that, which is why the industry answer is attribution, not prevention. Every screen carries a watermark with the viewer's identity, so a leak points back to one person. Here, let me screenshot it and read you the name.

2. "Why not just AES the file and email the password?"

Because a password can be forwarded, and once it's out you can never take it back. My key is split between the device and my server — I can revoke access after delivery, and expire it on a schedule. That's control after sending, which a password can't give you.

3. "What if I just patch out the licence check?"

There's nothing to patch. The licence isn't checked, it's used — it's half the decryption key. Patching the code leaves you holding the wrong key, and the authentication tag rejects every chunk.

4. "So it isn't really unbreakable?"

Correct, and nothing is. I've documented five attacker tiers and exactly which ones I stop. I fully stop T0 and T1, I substantially raise the cost for T2, and T3 needs trusted hardware, which I've written up as future work. That's the same way a vault door is rated.

5. "Why did you write it in Rust?"

Because the file parser eats hostile input, and Rust removes the entire class of memory bugs that live there. Not because it's harder to reverse-engineer — a stripped Rust binary is native code like any other. Memory safety and resistance to analysis are different properties.

If you remember nothing else

You can't control the attacker's computer — so don't try. Move the thing they need off their computer, and make everything they can reach worthless without it.
